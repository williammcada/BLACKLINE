# BLACKLINE: Last Exit — mini-game technical specification

**Version:** v0.1.0 · **Date:** 2026-10-01 · **Owner:** William McAda

**Status:** Design baseline accepted for staged development by the owner’s “proceed” instruction on 2026-10-01. Milestone A is the immediate implementation scope; subsequent milestones remain separate checkpoints. No game implemented, tested, released or deployed. BLACKLINE: Last Exit remains the working title. Numerical values are initial tuning values, not measured results.

**Product identity:** MathQuest cartridge, confirmed by the owner on 2026-10-01. The standalone racer is a development/playtest harness; the cartridge remains the product.

## 1. The game

Drive a stolen interceptor out of a sealed industrial city before its security forces destroy you. One continuous road climbs through refineries, dives into service tunnels, crosses broken elevated highways and ends in a desperate leap beyond the perimeter. The camera sits behind the car; the road bends and rises toward the horizon in an original 16-bit-inspired arcade presentation.

The player should alternate between three feelings: the pleasure of carrying speed through a clean bend, the panic of hearing pursuers close after a mistake, and the release of clearing a huge gap. Speed alone is insufficient. Brake, turn, straighten, accelerate and commit.

The requested requirements are one exciting track, tight turns, ramps across gaps, an OutRun-like driving perspective, dystopian art/music and lethal pursuing enemies. Further design choices form the accepted initial project baseline; tuning values remain adjustable through recorded milestone changes. No OutRun code, music, sprites, branding or track layout is required.

### Initial design decisions

| Element | v0.1.0 baseline |
| --- | --- |
| Format | Single-player point-to-point escape; one authored course, no laps |
| Duration | Approximately 4–5 minutes for a successful first clear; hard maximum 300 active seconds |
| Driving | Automatic acceleration, left/right steering, brake/drift and rechargeable boost |
| Combat | Enemies ram and shoot; player survives by driving, with no player weapon |
| Lives | One vehicle, 100 integrity; no respawn or checkpoint inside a round |
| Jumps | Automatic launch from ramps; speed and alignment determine success |
| Failure | Integrity reaches zero, falling into a gap, or 300 active seconds expire |
| Win | Reach the escape boundary after the final jump alive and within the limit |
| First deliverable | Standalone racer with direct Play and Retry; no mathematics or cartridge story system yet |
| Later integration | Separate MathQuest cartridge and Math Arcade host adapters |

The one-life proposal makes each terminal failure an unambiguous round end. A difficult section can still be tested directly using development tools; such tools must not appear as a free continuation path in the integrated game.

## 2. Track design

Working track name: **The Blackline Expressway**. Build one fixed, learnable road with controlled variation in enemy behavior. Required ramp locations, landing zones and bend sequences never randomize. Changes in skyline, color, elevation and musical intensity make one track feel like a journey.

The times below are pacing targets for a representative successful run, not timed teleports. World progress controls scenery and encounters; the 300-second clock runs separately. Tune course length against real completion times.

| Section | Target elapsed time | Driving and pursuit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Lockdown Avenue | 0–35 s | Wide boulevard; two readable bends; first interceptor appears after the player learns steering and braking |
| 2. Foundry Switchbacks | 35–85 s | Alternating tight bends, glowing furnaces, inside debris; brake before entry and accelerate through exit |
| 3. Broken Skyway | 85–130 s | First mandatory ramp and then a larger gap; broad landings; silhouettes of pursuers falling behind |
| 4. Freight Underpass | 130–180 s | Low ceilings, maintenance vehicles and sweeping bends; armored rammer enters; a brief recovery straight |
| 5. Siren Spiral | 180–235 s | Visually spiraling elevated road, tighter S-bends and one final mixed bend/launch setup; third pursuer permitted |
| 6. Last Exit | 235–285 s | Heavy pursuit, accelerating musical pulse, clearly marked final ramp and escape gate; victory immediately on safe escape |

The spiral is authored as a sequence of projected curves; it does not require a physically modeled corkscrew. There are three major mandatory gap jumps overall: two in Broken Skyway and the final escape jump. Section 5 uses a smaller elevation crest/ramp without an additional instant-death gap. Never put a blind mandatory gap immediately beyond a tight turn.

Each mandatory jump must have a visible approach, an alignment corridor, readable speed feedback and a generous landing straight. Protect that corridor from traffic spawns and enemy attacks from two seconds before takeoff until one second after landing. No mandatory jump may require a consumable boost charge.

## 3. Driving model and controls

Use forgiving arcade handling with meaningful corner entry speed. Avoid a car that merely slides left and right while the road animation plays. Road curvature creates outward drift proportional to speed squared; steering and braking counter it.

Simulation uses meters, seconds and radians internally. HUD speed is km/h. Begin tuning at a normal maximum of 220 km/h and boost maximum of 260 km/h, automatic acceleration of 8 m/s², braking deceleration of 20 m/s², and boost acceleration of 14 m/s². These values must be tuned together with track length and curve geometry.

Each curve has a signed curvature and an authored advisory speed. At or below that speed, ordinary steering can hold a clean line. Above it, increasing outward drift forces the driver to slow. Going modestly too fast should scrape the barrier rather than cause an unavoidable spin. Do not implement random loss of grip.

Brake plus steering produces a readable drift pose and tire trail. Drift is a controlled wider slide, not a separate timing minigame. Holding brake indefinitely substantially slows progress and lets pursuers approach. Releasing all controls resumes automatic acceleration only while the round is actively running; interruption pauses the entire simulation.

| Action | Keyboard | Touch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Steer | Left/Right or A/D | Two large adjacent left/right buttons, lower left |
| Brake/drift | Down or S | Large Brake button, lower right |
| Boost | Space | Boost button above/beside Brake, lower right |
| Pause | Escape or P | Persistent Pause button away from driving buttons |

Buttons must support steering plus brake/boost simultaneously. Opposite steering inputs cancel. Brake takes priority over boost; conflicting input must not burn charge. Boost reservoir starts full, provides up to 2 seconds at full charge, and refills over 10 seconds of active play. No boost recharge while paused. Releasing boost stops consumption. Airborne boost is disabled without consuming charge.

Use an initial minimum 64 CSS-pixel target for driving buttons, safe-area padding and readable normal fonts for directions. Landscape is the gameplay target on iPhone/iPad. Rotating to portrait pauses and presents a rotate-back message; menus remain usable. Do not force fullscreen as a prerequisite for Play.

## 4. Ramps and gaps

Jump initiation occurs when the car crosses the takeoff plane inside the marked ramp corridor. There is no jump button. Missing the corridor means entering the gap and falling, not receiving an invisible assisted launch.

Each jump defines takeoff position, corridor width, launch velocity, gap length, landing height and landing corridor. At takeoff, snapshot forward/lateral velocity. Use a simple ballistic vertical trajectory and continuous forward travel; limited airborne steering may trim lateral speed by no more than 15% of the grounded maximum. Do not silently snap the car to the landing center.

For equal-height prototype landings, flight time T = 2 × launchVerticalSpeed / gravity; forward range = forwardSpeed × T. Example initial geometry: gravity 20 m/s², vertical launch speed 10 m/s, flight time 1 s; a 40 m gap requires at least 144 km/h before adding the stated safety margin. General unequal-height landings must solve the trajectory against the actual landing plane, not reuse this shortcut. Ramp setup and displayed safe-speed threshold must be derived from the same geometry.

Target first-jump guidance around 160 km/h and final-jump guidance around 185 km/h, adjusting geometry to match. Display a speed band and prominent chevrons at least three seconds before the takeoff at maximum approach speed. Provide a pre-ramp straight long enough to reach the requirement without boost after ordinary corner braking. Verify this with a worst-case legal approach.

Successful landings give a short suspension bounce and sound. A modest edge landing may cost 10 integrity; a short jump or complete miss ends the run. Clearly show “Jump too slow” or “Missed landing” on the result screen. Pursuer projectiles and rams cannot damage an airborne player.

## 5. Pursuit that responds to driving

Use actual pursuer positions and speeds in road coordinates. A threat indicator may summarize proximity but must not secretly subtract health. Good driving creates a real gap; crashes lose speed and allow enemies to close. No teleporting into attack range or automatic damage for being slow.

At most three pursuers are active, with at most one attacking at a time. Their target speeds follow road conditions, visibility and difficulty tuning. They must brake for curves and use ramps; they may make mistakes. Do not give them perfect cornering or immunity to track hazards. If a replacement enters, use an authored visible entry behind the player, outside attack range, with at least two seconds of warning. A player who has escaped should enjoy that advantage until the next authored encounter.

| Enemy | Threat | Tell and counterplay |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interceptor | Lines up behind the player, then fires a short forward burst | Red aim lane and lock tone for at least 1.2 s; line locks for final 0.5 s; change lateral position |
| Armored rammer | Pulls alongside and commits to a lateral shove | Warning arrow and engine rev for at least 1 s; brake behind it or move away before commitment |

Begin with one interceptor, add a rammer in the underpass, and allow a second interceptor in the final third. Add complexity by combining existing threats with the road, not by introducing five enemy types. Pursuers that hit barriers visibly lose speed. Avoid synchronizing an attack with a required tight turn unless an independently tested escape path exists.

Provisional damage: barrier impact 12, traffic impact 18, ram 15, projectile burst 10, poor edge landing 10. Grant 0.8 seconds of damage immunity after a hit; a burst counts as one hit. Sustained wall contact causes no more than one barrier damage event per second. Resolve simultaneous collisions as one damage event using the largest applicable value. Impact speed loss is capped and applied once per contact event; holding the car against a wall must not multiply penalties every simulation tick.

Two clearly visible optional repair pickups restore 20 integrity each, capped at 100. Place them on reachable but less direct lines in recovery straights. Never require repairs to survive a clean run. No health decay, random critical hits or invisible catch meter.

## 6. Art, music and feedback

Original low-resolution pixel art: a 480 × 270 logical canvas, crisp scaling, layered distant skyline, segmented road, roadside billboards and large readable vehicle sprites. Perspective is pseudo-3D with hills and sprite scaling. Keep road edges and hazards brighter than scenery. Avoid a flat top-down view or a static neon backdrop.

Palette: soot black, oxidized teal, dirty violet, sodium amber and emergency red. Dystopia comes from shuttered towers, industrial smoke, surveillance signs and abandoned infrastructure. No gore or graphic injury; vehicle destruction communicates defeat. Use original fictional symbols and minimal text on scenery.

The player's interceptor needs visible steering poses, brake lights, boost exhaust, sparks and a damage state. Enemy silhouettes must remain distinguishable at speed. Provide shadows under jumping vehicles and a clear landing target. Motion effects must never obscure ramp markers or aim lanes.

Music direction: original industrial synth/chiptune at roughly 145–155 BPM, with a memorable bass motif, metallic percussion, dissonant pads and a rising final-section variation. Layer intensity by track section and nearby pursuit; maintain a coherent composition rather than a repeated warning beep. Add engine pitch, tires, lock warning, boost, takeoff, landing and collision sounds with independent music/SFX controls. Threat warnings remain visible with audio muted.

Audio begins only after a user gesture; pause/background suspends it and resume restores it without duplicated loops. Music defaults on after Play. Reduced-motion mode removes shake, strong flashes and excessive speed streaks while retaining driving information. No rapid full-screen strobes.

## 7. Screens and round lifecycle

Title: name, version, “A WILLIAM MCADA PRODUCT,” Play, Controls, Audio and reduced-motion setting. A concise pre-run instruction reads: “Brake for bends. Build speed for ramps. Keep the patrol behind you.”

HUD: integrity, speed, boost, route progress, remaining active time and rear-threat indicators. Avoid a second full rendered rear-view scene in v0.1.0; icons, distance and directional warnings provide the needed information.

State machine: title → ready → three-second countdown → running → result; running can enter paused and resume through a short countdown. Only running advances the active timer, enemies, cooldowns and physics. Failure or victory transitions once and stops simulation/input immediately. At the final movement step, crossing the finish before the 300-second deadline wins only if the car remains alive; otherwise the relevant failure wins. Use substep event times for boundary cases.

Results show outcome, reason, active duration, course percentage and collision count. Winning may show a three-level driving medal based on time and collisions; keep its thresholds in tuning data and label it as game performance, never mathematical mastery. No persistent leaderboard in the first prototype.

Standalone Retry creates a fresh round. Later host-controlled Retry requests a new round from the host and never launches locally without permission. Host pause, end and teardown always override local actions.

## 8. Technical architecture

Proposed implementation: browser JavaScript/TypeScript source modules with Canvas 2D pseudo-3D rendering and Web Audio. Build a self-contained HTML distribution with embedded assets plus an embeddable game module generated from the same source. No required CDN, account, server or paid dependency for the standalone racer. Hosted classroom operation is a separate host responsibility.

Use fixed 60 Hz simulation and an interpolated render loop. Cap catch-up steps; after a long stall, pause instead of advancing several invisible seconds. Perform swept collision checks across every road interval traversed so a fast car cannot skip a ramp, barrier, projectile or finish trigger. Deterministic seeded randomness supports replayable verification. The seed can vary attack timing within legal windows, not required road geometry.

Modules: lifecycle, shared input adapter, vehicle physics, road projection, track data, collision/jump system, pursuit director, audio, HUD, host adapter and verification harness. Keep tuning constants and authored encounters in data. Road segments contain distance bounds, signed curvature, elevation, width, scenery and hazard references. Jump and encounter definitions use stable IDs and distance ranges.

Prototype the projection and hills before final art. Clip segments against the near plane and nearer hills; project roadside sprites using the same camera transform as the road. Jump height is relative to track elevation, not screen pixels. The car's visible motion, hitbox and road position must agree.

Performance targets: smooth 60 fps on the chosen desktop baseline and at least stable 30 fps on physical target phones/tablets, with 60 fps the preferred result. Set final object/particle budgets from profiling; pool projectiles and particles, avoid per-frame asset decoding and cap rendering resolution. Candidate single-file budget: 12 MiB or less, with any justified exception documented. These are targets, not current performance claims.

## 9. Future cartridge and Arcade boundary

This specification delivers the racer first. A future MathQuest cartridge can frame it as smuggling evidence out of the city, with decisions before/after the drive. Do not implement narrative branches, currencies, shops, multiplayer or mathematics inside the driving engine now.

Proposed lifecycle contract: initialize(config), start(roundId, seed), pause(reason), resume(), end(reason), destroy(), and onResult(result). Result contains schema version, game ID, round ID, outcome, failure reason, active milliseconds, progress, collisions and seed. Emit exactly once per round. Validate start calls, reject duplicate active round IDs and remove event listeners/audio nodes during destroy. The eventual host adapter must reconcile this proposal with the real host API rather than assuming compatibility.

For Math Arcade, follow its current rule: one host math gate before each new round, no math interruption while driving, and a new gate after a terminal failure or victory before replay. Standalone testing intentionally has no gate. Native MathQuest narrative/gate placement will be specified in a later cartridge document; do not silently change existing cartridges.

At integration, reuse the approved shared math component under handbook S-03-M and record its exact revision/bundle hash. Do not recreate a new skill catalog. Host remains authoritative for math credit, round grants, learner evidence and refresh/reconnect rules. A refresh must not mint a free round. Durable resume/snapshot semantics are deferred until the actual host source is inspected.

## 10. Input reliability and storage

Apply approved U-10 from the first prototype. Track each contact by stable identity; share one input implementation across distributions. Release/cancel/capture loss, missed-release recovery, backgrounding, focus loss, lock, rotation, pause, result, retry and teardown must clear held actions. Require fresh intentional input afterward. Do not use an inactivity timeout that interrupts a genuinely held finger.

Prevent gameplay text selection, native callouts, context menus, drag previews and accidental gesture zoom across the HUD, world and controls. Preserve ordinary interaction and accessible zoom in menus/settings. Fit the game from the current viewport and safe areas without accumulating scale changes. Check browser-bar changes and later math-keyboard open/close independently. An intercepted context menu must pause and clear controls.

v0.1.0 stores no progress, learner identity, scores or settings across reload; settings last only for the current page session. U-09 persistent-work deletion is therefore not applicable to this prototype. If persistence is added, implement visible scoped deletion, confirmation/cancel and documented recovery behavior as part of that same change. Do not use incidental localStorage as an unreviewed shortcut.

## 13. Verification and acceptance

All implementation/device checks are currently **Not run**, because no implementation exists. Record actual commit, artifact hash, browser/OS/device and evidence per result. Physical-device checks cannot be replaced by simulated events.

| Required check | Acceptance evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Handling | Full clean runs show meaningful braking, recoverable modest overspeed and no constant-steering shortcut through every bend |
| Jump boundaries | Under-speed, corridor misses, edge landings and safe-speed cases match geometry; all three mandatory jumps clear without boost |
| Pursuit fairness | Clean driving increases separation; repeated crashes allow lethal attacks; no unseen damage or unavoidable combined attack |
| Collision stability | Wall holds and overlapping attacks cannot drain integrity per frame; high-speed sweeps catch every trigger |
| Timing | Only active play consumes 300 s; pause/stall cannot secretly advance; victory/failure at the boundary is deterministic |
| Lifecycle | Repeated play, death, retry, pause and teardown produce no duplicated timers, listeners, results or music |
| Input release | Sustained multi-touch, both release orders, drag outside, capture failure, cancellation, lock and background return recover correctly |
| Viewport | Rapid taps, browser bars, rotation and return leave scale, touch mapping and required controls stable |
| Native menus | Long-press world/HUD/control text and images produce no disruptive selection/callout/menu |
| Device matrix | Physical iPhone 15 Pro and iPad Safari; iPhone Edge where available; desktop Chrome/Edge keyboard. Record actual versions; do not infer one browser from another |
| Distribution | Standalone file and hosted build, when provided, identify the same version and show identical input behavior; offline desktop standalone loads embedded assets |
| Accessibility/audio | Muted play retains all warnings; reduced motion preserves hazard information; pause/resume and fresh retries restore sound correctly |
| Final difficulty review | Observe at least five fresh attempts across two players; log section failure causes and completion times, then tune unavoidable or unreadable failure patterns |

Physical iOS opening of a downloaded HTML file is not promised; browser-hosted play is the intended iOS path. A desktop standalone test is not evidence for the iOS file-opening workflow. Release-blocking input, viewport or native-menu defects must be fixed for the affected supported device; unperformed checks stay explicitly Not run.

The first build should succeed at one thing before content expands: driving a tight curve, accelerating toward a visible gap and landing while an enemy loses ground should already feel exciting.
